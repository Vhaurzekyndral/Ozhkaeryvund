# Ozhkaeryvund

> The moon's last thread passes through a loom carved from a sleeping dragon's horn.

## The Room Above the Stair

The staircase ends one step below the room. Visitors cross the remaining distance without noticing until they try to leave. Then the gap appears, narrow as a finger, with a pale sky moving slowly beneath it.

Inside stands a loom made of white horn. Its cloth has no color while the shutters are open. Close them, and a landscape appears: seven dark hills surrounding a lake, a house on the nearest shore, and a road that enters the water without becoming wet.

The weaver works only after someone has fallen asleep in the chair beside her. She does not ask visitors to sleep. She brings tea, places a blanket within reach, and waits until their answers begin arriving more slowly than her questions.

The chair remembers each occupant as a small warm patch beneath its wooden seat.

## A Visitor Carrying an Unused Spindle

A man arrived with a spindle wrapped in black cloth. He had found it inside the wall of a house he was repairing. Around it was wound a thread too fine to see directly, though its shadow could be followed across the table.

The weaver held it near the loom. In the landscape, a shutter opened on the lakeside house. Someone placed a lamp in its window. The man recognized the lamp's chipped blue base and the way its flame leaned toward the room instead of the glass.

He remembered carrying that lamp upstairs as a child. He remembered somebody asking him to leave it beside a closed door. Beyond the door had come the sound of a spinning wheel, although his house had never contained one.

The weaver placed the spindle in his lap and asked whether the door had ever opened. He looked toward the chair before answering.

## A Lake Beneath the Fabric

When he slept, the cloth grew heavier. Water gathered along its lower edge, hanging in drops that reflected the room from above. The weaver caught one in a silver thimble and set it beside the sleeping man's hand.

Within the drop, the seven hills moved. They were the ridges of a folded wing. Beneath the lake rested an enormous eye, its closed lid covered in reeds. The road crossed the creature's brow and continued toward a place beyond the cloth.

The spindle turned once in the man's lap. The invisible thread passed through the thimble, through the drop, and into the lakeside house. Its lamp brightened.

At the far side of the room, the missing stair became visible as a long bridge. Beneath it, the pale sky had acquired a single small moon.

## What the Horn Had Held

The weaver's loom was older than the room. She had found its horn frame standing in a field after a storm. Grass beneath it lay flat in the shape of a sleeping animal, and the rain around it fell upward for three days.

She had brought it home piece by piece. During the journey, each piece became something familiar: a chair leg, a walking stick, the handle of a cupboard. Only when assembled did the horn remember its curve.

The first cloth she wove showed the room she was sitting in. The second showed the same room without her. The third showed a lake with a house beside it, and she had never managed to remove it from the loom.

Now, as the visitor slept, she saw herself in the house's open window. She was younger there, holding a spindle wrapped in black cloth.

## A Pattern That Refused Its Name

Marks appeared along the loom's upper beam:

*Ozh — kaer — yvund — eshial.*

The weaver counted them with the silver thimble. Each touch produced a sound from the visitor's closed door: a wheel turning, a foot shifting, a hand resting on wood.

The sleeping man opened one palm. In it lay a little blue flame without a lamp. The invisible thread passed through the flame and became visible, bright enough to cast a narrow shadow across the floor.

In the cloth, the lakeside door opened. No person emerged. A warm patch appeared on the road, then another, as though someone were walking toward the lake carrying the weight of an empty chair.

The great eye beneath the water remained closed. One reed bent slowly across its lid, pointing toward the moon beneath the missing stair.

## The Cloth Left on the Loom

The visitor woke before dawn. The spindle in his lap was bare. He could remember dreaming of a staircase, but could no longer remember which side of its closed door he had been standing on.

The weaver returned the black cloth to him. It was damp along one edge and smelled faintly of lamp oil. He folded it carefully, placed it in his coat, and crossed the missing step without looking down.

After he left, she opened the shutters. The landscape vanished as usual, but the cloth retained the weight of water. Beneath the chair, a new warm patch spread slowly into the shape of a small lake.

She has not closed the shutters since.

At night, the loom turns silver in the moonlight. A thread too fine to see moves through its horn frame, and somewhere behind a door that has finally opened, a wheel continues turning without a sound.
